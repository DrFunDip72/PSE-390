## The Six Steps

Memorize the order and what each step is called. Questions 1–5 test exactly this, and Q30–33 apply it.

> **From class** In class (Sep 21–23) he stressed Steps 2, 4 and 5 (define the issue, lenses, Option C) and called the gut check "spidey sense," sometimes "the Spirit." He sometimes counted eight steps; the handout has six, and the study guide follows the handout.

1. **Gather the Facts**. Always first. Know what actually happened before judging it.
2. **Define the Ethical Issues**. Name the type of dilemma using the 13 categories below.
3. **Identify the Affected Parties (Stakeholders)**. Everyone touched, including people with no voice, like the contract workers who absorb the overtime (Q3).
4. **Identify the Obligations & Consequences (Lenses)**. The tool for this step is the ethical lenses in the Ethics Toolkit, not a cost-benefit analysis (Q2).
5. **Think Creatively About Potential Actions (Option C)**. Escape the either/or. Recall vs. say nothing becomes a free patch plus a plain notice (Q5).
6. **Check Your Gut (Spider Senses; The Spirit)**. Last step. If every lens says yes but you feel uneasy, pause and re-examine before acting (Q4).

Memory hook: Facts → Issues → People → Lenses → Option C → Gut. Textbook (MBE Ch. 2) calls its version "Eight Steps." The course handout is the Six Steps; use the Six on the exam.

## The 13 ethical issue types

Source: [Defining Ethical Issues – Succinct](https://byu.instructure.com/courses/38393/files/13848250) for the definitions, and the longer [defining ethical issues](https://byu.instructure.com/courses/38393/files/13510254) handout for the "ask yourself" questions. Match the scenario to its *single best* fit; the exam gives four choices.

> **From class** In class (Sep 14) he gave a real company example for each type, listed in [lecture-notes.md](lecture-notes.md) (Boeing, Wells Fargo, McKinsey, HP, Amazon, J&J, and more). He ranked Skirting the Rules as "slightly less bad" than Playing Dirty.

| Issue | What it is | Ask yourself |
|---|---|---|
| Standing Up to Power | Someone with authority over you asks you to do something you believe is wrong. | Do they have the right to ask this of me? Are there others who agree with me? |
| Made a Promise and World Has Changed | Circumstances shifted after you committed, so the promise is now costly or hard to keep. | Can I still keep it, even at cost? Does keeping it make other promises harder to keep? |
| Intervention | You see wrongdoing and must decide whether and how to step in despite risk to yourself. | Am I the right person, or should I recruit help? How do I cause the least harm to everyone? |
| Conflict of Interest | Your personal stake in an outcome competes with your duty to act objectively. | Who has a right to know, and have I told them? Would my actions make others question my motives? Are there rules I should follow? |
| Suspicions Without Enough Evidence | You sense something is wrong but lack proof solid enough to act on. | What happens if I act and the allegation is false? Who is accountable for solving the problem? |
| Playing Dirty | Getting a fair outcome seems to require using unfair or unethical means yourself. | Would this really bring justice or the greatest good? Does harming the other party help anyone besides me? |
| Skirting the Rules | A worthwhile goal seems to require bending or breaking a rule or law. | Would those with authority over the rule want me to break it here? What are all the reasons the rule matters? |
| Dissemblance | Concealing or shading the truth might produce a better outcome than full disclosure. | Do I have the authority to reveal the truth? What are my real reasons for misrepresenting it? |
| Loyalty | You must decide how much you owe someone because of the relationship, even at a cost. | Have they shown loyalty to me? Would they want my loyalty if they knew all the costs? |
| Sacrificing Personal Values or Relationships | Staying true to your principles will impose a real cost on someone else. | What costs am I willing to bear for my beliefs or relationships? What burden am I placing on others? |
| Unfair Advantage | You have a genuine edge over someone, but you are not sure it was fairly earned. | What makes the advantage unfair? If I were the other party, how would I view someone making my choice? |
| Repair | You made a mistake and could get away with leaving it unfixed. | Who should bear the cost of the mistake? Did I act unethically when I made it? |
| Showing Mercy | Someone asks you to ease or forgive a consequence that is yours to enforce. | Is mercy mine to give? Who bears the cost of showing it? |

### Pairs that get confused

**Standing Up to Power vs. Skirting the Rules.** Power = a person above you *tells* you to do wrong (Q6). Skirting = *you* are tempted to bend a rule for a good goal (Q31, Kim's $15,000 limit).**Conflict of Interest vs. Loyalty.** COI = your own stake threatens objectivity in a duty you hold (Q9, scholarship committee). Loyalty = what you owe a person because of the relationship (Q11, mentor).**Suspicions vs. Intervention.** Suspicions = you lack proof (Q13). Intervention = you know it is happening and must decide whether to step in (Q30).**Playing Dirty vs. Unfair Advantage.** Playing Dirty = *you* consider unethical means to even things up (Q12). Unfair Advantage = you *already hold* an edge you doubt you earned (Q32).**Dissemblance vs. Suspicions.** Dissemblance = you know the truth and are tempted to hide or soften it (Q7, Q14, Q33). Suspicions = you do not know the truth.**Showing Mercy vs. Repair.** Mercy = someone else's consequence is yours to enforce (Q10). Repair = your own mistake you could leave unfixed.

## The Ethics Toolkit

Source: [Ethics Toolkit.pdf](https://byu.instructure.com/courses/38393/files/13510253). Five theories, ten decision criteria. Know the criterion name, the theory it belongs to, and the question that goes with it. The [key questions](https://byu.instructure.com/courses/38393/files/13510276) handout is the one-page version.

> **From class** He said (Sep 16) the exam will describe a scenario and you name the framework. He also said the frameworks overlap, and that the same case can be read through more than one.

| Theory | Criterion | An action is ethical when… | Ask yourself |
|---|---|---|---|
| Deontology · Immanuel Kant | Universality | I would be willing for it to become universal law. | What if everyone did it? What obligations do I have? |
| Deontology · Immanuel Kant | Dignity | It treats others as ends in themselves, not just as a means. | Am I protecting the rights and dignity of other people? |
| Deontology · Immanuel Kant | Agency | It allows other people to make their own choices. | Am I giving others the opportunity to make their own decisions? |
| Deontology · Immanuel Kant | Justice | Benefits and costs are equitably distributed. | Will this be fair to all involved? |
| Utilitarianism · John Stuart Mill | Utility | It maximizes happiness and minimizes pain; the greatest benefit to the most people. | Who is affected? Who benefits and who is harmed? |
| Virtue Ethics · Aristotle | Personal virtues | It helps me become a better person. | What kind of person would this make me? What would the best person in the world do? |
| Virtue Ethics · Aristotle | Disclosure | I would be willing to have it broadcast to the public. | Would I want this broadcast to the world? Can I live with it? ("Front page of the newspaper") |
| Virtue Ethics · Aristotle | Integrity / eudaimonism | It is true to who I am and who I want to become. | What action best reflects the person I want to be? Does it pass my "sleep test"? |
| Social Contracts · Locke, Rousseau, Rawls | Social obligations | I am fulfilling my obligation to contribute to a harmonious society. | Am I fulfilling my societal duties? What kind of world do I want to live in? |
| Ethics of Care · Feminist philosophy | Care | It meets the needs of the most vulnerable. | Who is vulnerable here? Who does not have a voice who should? |
| Ethics of Care · Feminist philosophy | Relationships | It benefits those I have trust in and obligations toward. | Who has put their trust in me? |

### How to match fast

**Trigger phrase → criterion**

- "What if everyone did it?" → **Universality** (Deontology)
- "Used / just a means" → **Dignity** (Deontology)
- "Let them decide for themselves" → **Agency** (Deontology)
- "Greatest good for the most people" → **Utility**
- "Front page / broadcast" → **Disclosure** (Virtue)
- "Sleep test / kind of person" → **Integrity** / personal virtues
- "Who is vulnerable / no voice" → **Care**
- "Who trusts me" → **Relationships** (Care)
- "Societal duties / kind of world" → **Social obligations**

**Typical lens conflicts**

- **Utilitarianism vs. Care.** Closing a free clinic to benefit more patients: Utilitarianism leans yes, Care leans no (Q19).
- **Utilitarianism vs. Deontology.** Profit or "greater good" does not license using people as means (Q22).
- **Disclosure** belongs to *Virtue Ethics*, not Deontology or Social Contracts (Q20).
- **Justice** belongs to *Deontology* in this toolkit (Q20 trap).

## Moral rationalization

Source: [rationalization handout](https://byu.instructure.com/courses/38393/files/13510256). Definition: convincing yourself that your unethical behavior somehow does not violate your ethical standards; more specifically, suspending the self-sanctions that normally guide your behavior. Eleven forms.

> **From class** He said (Oct 5) "these rationalization ideas will be on the test": expect a few statements and you say which rationalization each one is. When a statement fits two, he accepts either. Necessity is "no choice"; higher loyalty is a noble purpose.

| Form | Handout definition | What it sounds like |
|---|---|---|
| Denial of responsibility | Shifting blame to another party. | "Talk to my manager, she told me to." (Q24) |
| Denial of injury | Arguing that no one was hurt. | "Nobody will miss a few pens." (Q25) |
| Denial of victim | Implying the unethical behavior was the victim's fault. | "They had it coming." |
| Condemnation of condemners | Turning the judgment onto the judge. | "You've never run a business a day in your life." (Q26) |
| Appeal to higher loyalty | Acting in the name of a higher purpose; the end justifies the means. | "Saving 350 families is a higher calling." (Q-Medica) |
| Moral ledger | Believing you have done enough good to offset or justify the bad. | "We give 10% to charity, so this is no big deal." (Q27) |
| Defense of necessity | Having no choice but to engage in the misconduct. | "I had no choice." (Q29) |
| Claim of normalcy | Appealing to industry or local norms. | "Everyone does it." "Standard industry practice." |
| Denial of negative intent | Arguing that pure motives make the action justifiable. | "My heart was in the right place." (Q29) |
| Advantageous comparison | Pointing to an extreme case so your behavior looks mild. | "Compared to Enron, this is nothing." (Q28) |
| Euphemistic language | Swapping harsh words for morally neutral ones to downplay the act. | "Tariff optimization." "Calibration variance." (Q29) |

### Look-alikes

**Denial of injury vs. Denial of victim.** Injury = no one was harmed. Victim = harm happened but the victim deserved it.**Denial of responsibility vs. Denial of negative intent.** Responsibility = someone else is to blame. Intent = I am to blame but I meant well.**Claim of normalcy vs. Advantageous comparison.** Normalcy = it is common, so it is fine. Comparison = it is minor next to something far worse.**Moral ledger vs. Appeal to higher loyalty.** Ledger = past or separate good deeds offset this. Higher loyalty = a cause served by this very act justifies it.**Defense of necessity vs. Appeal to higher loyalty.** Necessity = I had no alternative. Higher loyalty = a noble purpose outweighs the rule. If the quote is about a cause (families, mission), it is loyalty; if it is about being cornered, it is necessity.

## Cases and scenarios from class

These are in Canvas under Files › Course Materials and the Defining Ethical Issues Content folder. They are not on the study guide, but the exam uses the same skill: name the issue, name the lens. Issue labels here are a student's reading, not the professor's, so treat them as practice.

#### Q-Medica Launch [PDF](https://byu.instructure.com/courses/38393/files/13976225)

Apex Health's CardioScan Q has a rare telemetry glitch. Shipping saves 350 jobs; fixing it means bankruptcy. The assignment asks students to identify rationalizations in six executive statements. This is the best practice for Part 4.

| Speaker | Statement | Rationalization |
|---|---|---|
| Mark (CEO) | "My hands are tied; I just have to execute what ownership demands." | Denial of responsibility |
| David (CFO) | "Nobody is actually going to die or get permanently hurt by a 10-second blip." | Denial of injury |
| Elena (Engineer) | "Every medical device company cuts corners… standard industry practice." | Claim of normalcy |
| Sarah (QA) | "350 families… a much higher calling than fussing over a microscopic variance." | Appeal to higher loyalty |
| Mark (CEO) | "We spend millions manufacturing free equipment for rural clinics." | Moral ledger |
| Tom (Legal) | Call it a "minor calibration variance" instead of a hardware defect. | Euphemistic language |

Lens view: Utilitarianism weighs 350 jobs against patient risk; Deontology says patients cannot be used as a means (dignity, agency); Care points to surgical patients who cannot know the risk. Option C: disclose to hospitals and regulators, seek bridge financing, or ship only with a warning and a fast fix.

#### The Advisor in the Boardroom [PDF](https://byu.instructure.com/courses/38393/files/14038407)

Sam Reyes is an advisor and board observer at Brightline. The rival, Vector, offers him a Chief Revenue Officer job. As an observer he is not a legal fiduciary and California non-competes are generally unenforceable, so the question is what he *ethically* owes. Decision point 2 flips to Brightline's CEO, who learns of the talks after Sam advised on confidential matters.

**Issues:** Conflict of Interest (the core), Loyalty, Dissemblance (his assurance that he'd "made too much money" and the silence during talks). **Handout questions that fit:** Who has a right to know and have I told them? Would my actions cause others to question my motives? **Lenses:** Disclosure ("would I want these talks broadcast?"), Social Contracts (implicit trust and confidentiality), Relationships (who has trusted him). This case matches the Oct 12 Conflicts of Interest class.
